# Afterlight — Product Foundation

**Working product promise:** *A whole new world, in 15 minutes.*  
**Product category:** Short-form fiction entertainment  
**Format:** Original, AI-assisted fiction designed for approximately 10–15 minutes per story  
**Business model:** Free, ad-supported access plus a paid subscription  
**Status:** Early concept; assumptions require user validation

---

## 1. Core experience

### The job Afterlight does

When someone has a short break, wants to unwind, or feels like being entertained, Afterlight helps them find a compelling fictional story without having to search through a huge catalogue.

### The intended journey

1. **Arrive:** The reader opens Afterlight with a general desire to be entertained, not necessarily a specific title in mind.
2. **Discover:** The home screen offers a small number of relevant, appealing choices.
3. **Commit:** A clear premise and reading-time estimate make it easy to decide.
4. **Immerse:** The reader enters a calm reading experience with progress saved automatically.
5. **Complete:** The story delivers a satisfying ending and the reader can rate or save it.
6. **Return:** A relevant new story, connected universe, or optional recommendation gives the reader a reason to revisit.

### Experience target

- A reader can identify a story worth trying in about 10 seconds.
- Stories are designed to take roughly 10–15 minutes, while actual reading time varies by reader and story.
- Readers can leave and resume without losing their place.
- A completed story feels like a complete experience, not an unfinished engagement loop.

**Core promise:** Find a world. Live a story. Come back when you want another.

## 2. Visual identity

Afterlight should feel like entering a fictional world—not opening a generic AI tool or an endless social feed.

| Element | Direction | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Background | Deep charcoal / warm black | Creates an atmospheric, low-distraction canvas |
| Primary accent | Muted amber | Adds warmth and a recognisable visual signature |
| Secondary surface | Warm cream | Keeps reading comfortable and literary |
| Story titles | Expressive, elegant serif | Gives fiction a distinct editorial identity |
| Interface text | Clear, modern sans-serif | Keeps navigation and controls easy to understand |
| Story artwork | Cinematic, genre-aware imagery | Helps readers understand the mood before opening a story |
| Cards | Large, editorial compositions | Makes discovery feel curated rather than crowded |
| Motion | Subtle transitions and scene changes | Adds polish without distracting from reading |

### Avoid

- Generic AI-product gradients and excessive glass effects.
- Dense grids of tiny story cards.
- Too many competing buttons or badges.
- Motion that interrupts reading or slows navigation.
- Visual tricks that disguise ads as editorial recommendations.

The interface should be distinctive, but legibility, accessibility, and reading comfort take priority over decoration.

## 3. Core screens

### 3.1 Home — the discovery engine

**Goal:** Help the reader find a story quickly without presenting an overwhelming catalogue.

Suggested hierarchy:

1. Afterlight wordmark and a simple account/library entry point.
2. A contextual line such as **“What world are we entering today?”**
3. One featured story with strong artwork, a compelling hook, genre, and reading time.
4. A **Continue reading** row for unfinished stories.
5. A small **Picked for you** selection based on stated preferences and feedback.
6. Genre exploration: Mystery, Romance, Sci-fi, Thriller, Fantasy, Comedy, and other supported genres.
7. A **Newly imagined** or fresh-release section.

The home screen should prioritise a few high-confidence recommendations over endless scrolling.

### 3.2 Story preview — sell the premise, not the plot

Every story preview should communicate:

- Cinematic cover artwork.
- Story title.
- A short, spoiler-free hook.
- Genre and mood.
- Estimated reading time.
- Relevant content information or age guidance where needed.
- A clear **Start reading** action.

**Illustrative example:**

> **THE LAST TRAIN TO VARANASI**  
> Every night, a train arrives at Platform 9. Nobody remembers boarding it. Nobody who leaves remembers where they went.  
> *Mystery · Supernatural · 12 min*

The hook should create curiosity without misleading the reader or revealing the ending.

### 3.3 Reading screen — the most important interface

**Goal:** Make it comfortable to stay with the story because the story is engaging—not because the interface makes leaving difficult.

Core requirements:

- A distraction-free reading layout.
- Comfortable font size, line length, contrast, and line spacing.
- Reader-controlled font size and theme preferences.
- A subtle reading-progress indicator.
- Automatic progress saving and reliable resume behaviour.
- Bookmark/save controls that do not interrupt the text.
- Accessible navigation and support for different screen sizes.
- Optional full-screen reading mode.

Do not force every paragraph or arbitrary word count onto a separate screen. Let the story flow naturally. Use scene breaks where the narrative calls for them, not to manufacture taps.

### 3.4 Completion screen — make the ending matter

After a reader finishes, acknowledge the experience before recommending anything else.

Include:

- A calm completion message, such as **“And that's how the story ends.”**
- A simple rating or feedback action.
- Save to library.
- A spoiler-free share card.
- One strong, relevant next recommendation.
- Related stories or the next episode when the story is part of a series.

Avoid immediately forcing an ad, autoplaying another story, or presenting an aggressive chain of recommendations.

### 3.5 Library — a personal collection of worlds

The library should make it easy to find:

- Currently reading.
- Saved stories.
- Completed stories.
- Favourite series or connected universes.
- Reading history.

A reader's library should be useful and personal, with controls to remove items and manage history.

## 4. Retention strategy

### Retention thesis

**Do not optimise for making people stay longer in one session. Optimise for making the experience good enough that they willingly return.**

Five retention mechanisms to test:

| Mechanism | Product expression | Why it may help |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Personalised discovery | Relevant stories based on declared interests and feedback | Reduces search effort and improves fit |
| Fresh stories | A dependable cadence of genuinely good new releases | Gives readers something new to discover |
| Connected story universes | Recurring characters, locations, themes, or timelines across separate stories | Rewards curiosity and creates continuity |
| Personal library | Saved, completed, and in-progress stories | Helps readers build a meaningful collection |
| Optional daily recommendation | One carefully selected **12-Minute Story of the Day** | Provides a lightweight reason to return without pressure |

### The 12-Minute Story of the Day

A daily editorial-style recommendation could become a recognisable Afterlight ritual. It should be optional, easy to dismiss, and available without a punitive streak. Missing a day should never create guilt or make the reader lose progress.

### Healthy retention guardrails

- No punitive streaks or loss-aversion mechanics.
- No fake urgency or misleading countdowns.
- No notifications whose only purpose is to pull a reader back into the app.
- Let readers choose notification frequency and turn notifications off easily.
- Do not make it difficult to pause, leave, or delete an account.
- Recommend stories for relevance and quality, not solely for predicted session length.

Retention is an outcome of value delivered. It is not permission to manipulate attention.

## 5. Monetisation

Afterlight can combine free access supported by advertising with a paid subscription. The monetisation model must preserve trust and immersion.

### Free plan — ad-supported

Potential benefits:

- Access to a curated selection of stories.
- Personalised recommendations.
- Basic library and reading progress.
- Clearly labelled ads in appropriate, non-reading placements.

### Premium plan — subscription

Potential benefits to test:

- Ad-free experience.
- Access to premium stories, collections, or selected series.
- Early access to selected releases.
- Enhanced personalisation or reader controls where these provide real value.

### Advertising rules

- Do not interrupt a story mid-reading with an ad.
- Prefer clearly labelled placements on discovery screens or between completed stories.
- Never disguise paid placements as organic recommendations.
- Keep ad frequency low enough that free readers can still enjoy the product.
- Make the difference between free and premium clear before a purchase decision.

### Pricing principle

Do not invent a price before testing the target audience, perceived value, local purchasing power, content costs, and ad economics. Validate willingness to pay with real reader feedback and experiments.

## 6. Product metrics

### North Star Metric

**Completed stories per weekly active reader.**

This is a proposed North Star, not a final decision. It connects product usage to the core promise: readers finding and finishing entertaining stories.

### Supporting metrics

| Metric | What it helps answer |
| --- | --- |
| First-story start rate | Does discovery give new readers a reason to begin? |
| Story completion rate | Are readers reaching the ending? |
| Reader satisfaction after completion | Was the story actually worth the reader's time? |
| D1 retention | Does the first experience encourage a voluntary return? |
| D7 retention | Are readers finding repeat value over a week? |
| Stories completed per week | How much finished-story value are active readers getting? |
| Recommendation click-through | Are recommendations relevant enough to open? |
| Premium conversion | Are readers willing to pay for the premium value? |
| Ad revenue per free active reader | Can the free plan be sustainable without excessive ads? |

### Interpret metrics carefully

Average session time is not a sufficient success measure. A long session could mean someone is enjoying several stories—or struggling to find one. Pair behavioural metrics with story ratings, reader feedback, completion, and return behaviour.

Track cohorts and compare new readers with returning readers. Avoid interpreting a single metric in isolation.

## 7. Differentiation and product positioning

### The key distinction

Afterlight is **not positioned as an AI story generator**. AI is a production tool that can help create, adapt, and personalise stories. The reader-facing product is an entertainment platform where people discover consistently engaging fictional worlds.

Readers should not have to think about prompts, models, or generation pipelines. They should care about the premise, characters, pacing, writing quality, and ending.

### What must be excellent

- Strong opening hooks.
- Believable characters with clear motivations.
- Coherent plots and satisfying endings.
- Good pacing at a short-fiction length.
- Distinct voices across genres.
- Continuity and consistency inside connected story universes.
- Editorial quality checks before stories reach readers.
- Appropriate content labels and moderation.

### The real competitive advantage to build

The advantage will not come merely from generating more text. It will come from combining:

1. A reliable system for producing and reviewing good fiction.
2. A deep understanding of what different readers enjoy.
3. A discovery experience that gets readers to the right story quickly.
4. A recognisable editorial identity and catalogue of memorable story worlds.
5. A reading experience that respects the reader's time.

**Positioning line:** *Open Afterlight. Find a world. Live a story. Come back for another.*

## MVP focus

The first version should validate the central experience before expanding into complex features.

### Build first

- Home/discovery screen.
- Story preview screen.
- Immersive reading screen.
- Automatic reading progress and resume.
- Completion screen with lightweight feedback.
- Basic personal library.
- A small, curated catalogue of quality short stories.
- Basic analytics for starts, completions, satisfaction, and voluntary returns.

### Defer until validated

- Large-scale automated story generation.
- Complex social features, comments, or public profiles.
- Heavy gamification.
- Complicated recommendation systems before enough useful data exists.
- Multiple subscription tiers before one clear premium proposition is validated.
- Autoplay or endless-feed mechanics.

---

## Open questions to validate

1. Which initial audience has the strongest appetite for 10–15 minute fiction?
2. Which genres create the strongest first-session satisfaction and repeat reading?
3. Do readers prefer standalone stories, episodic stories, or connected universes?
4. What story length feels right across genres and reading contexts?
5. What is the minimum editorial quality bar before a generated story is published?
6. Which ad placements are acceptable without damaging the experience?
7. What premium benefits would readers genuinely pay for?
8. Which notification, if any, do readers find useful rather than intrusive?

## Decision rule

When choosing between two features, prefer the one that makes it easier for readers to **discover a good story, enjoy it, finish it, or willingly return**. If a feature only increases time spent without improving reader value, it should not be prioritised.
